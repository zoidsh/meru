# Where a word comes from

Every word in every language, in the glossary, a style sheet or a sidecar, comes from the first of these sources that has it, and says which. `SKILL.md`, `glossary.md` and the style sheets link here rather than restate it.

1. **The operating system's own words**, meaning its labels (File, Paste, Quit, Save As…) and the names of its parts (menu bar, Dock, Finder, System Settings, Do Not Disturb, system tray), come from the platform showing them: Apple on macOS, Microsoft on Windows, GNOME on Linux. An entry whose `msgctxt` is `macOS`, `Windows` or `Linux` is such a label, and takes its word from the style sheet's Platform words (Apple Mail's for a mail menu item). A part only macOS has, such as the menu bar or the Dock, takes Apple's name in Meru's own copy too.
2. **Gmail's own terms** take Gmail's word in that language: a folder, a Gmail action, the text of a toast over Gmail, so the two read the same side by side. The words are under the glossary's [Gmail's words](glossary.md#gmails-words) and [Move email to Trash](glossary.md#move-email-to-trash); a Gmail term missing there takes Gmail's own word too. Gmail's register does not come with it: the style sheet's address form still applies, so German keeps _du_ where Gmail writes _Sie_.
3. **Google's names** take Google's word; the Workspace apps' names stay English, under the glossary's [Terms that stay English](glossary.md#terms-that-stay-english).
4. **Meru's own copy**, everything else, takes, in order:
   1. (4a) Chrome's word where Chrome has the term, read from Chromium's translations at the Chromium version of the repository's Electron;
   2. (4b) a recorded call of the user's, which overrides Chrome only as a named exception with its reason; there are none now;
   3. (4c) otherwise the word most commonly used for that concept in popular apps in that language, counted across a fixed set with one vote each: Thunderbird, Firefox (Mozilla's l10n repositories), VS Code (`microsoft/vscode-loc`), Android (AOSP's `values-<lang>/strings.xml` of core apps such as Settings, SystemUI, Messaging and Email), LibreOffice (its translations repository), the Microsoft Terminology Collection (the TBX) and Apple's macOS apps (this Mac's `.loctable`s; one vote, counted as an app, not as an authority). The concept is counted as Meru uses it, in the narrowest sense that matches, such as an unread count on an app icon for a badge, never a looser sense that mixes in another meaning. Each distinct message counts once, for the candidate word it matches longest, and each app's vote is the word it uses most; the word with most votes wins, marked `agent-chosen` with its tally, such as _Hinweis: 4 of 7 (Thunderbird, Firefox, Android, Microsoft)_. A word that another of Meru's concepts already uses in that language, such as its word for a notification, a toast, a badge or an update, is out of the vote and of the direct match below, and so is a message it matches longest. A direct match names the same thing Meru shows: a banner or strip form (Banner, baner, bannière, faixa, tira) is none for a toast, a transient message in the window, and Meru keeps its banner word for Gmail's banners. When two concepts compete for one word, the concept decided by stronger evidence keeps it and the other takes its next step, unless the stronger one has a free word at its own step, when neither has to lose: in Brazilian Portuguese the toast, weaker and unresolved, keeps its existing aviso, and the badge takes Thunderbird's free direct match, emblema. When the winner has 2 votes or fewer, the vote ties, or fewer than 3 of the 7 have the concept, the fallback is, in this order: Google's own wording for the concept (Gmail, Google One, Google Workspace, Google Calendar, Chrome or Android Help, with `hl=<tag>`), then the counted word in the app message that matches Meru's concept most directly, and only if nothing remains, the existing word, marked unresolved with each app's word, for a speaker to settle.

Apple is never the authority for a word in Meru's own copy; its macOS apps are one vote of seven in step 4c. Grammar is not a word: address form, capitalization, label form, punctuation and the ellipsis follow Apple's conventions for each language, as its style sheet says. A rule backed by Chrome or by a call of the user's is `wrong` in every sidecar; an agent-chosen word is `wrong` too, since the glossary is the decision; `doubtful` is for a heuristic that can misfire, which the reviewer confirms or dismisses. [How a source is cited](#how-a-source-is-cited) says how each source is cited.

## How a source is cited

Each word carries its source in brackets: **(Chrome n/N)**, Chrome's word in n of the N messages that have the term, counted in Chromium 156's `generated_resources`, `components_strings` and `ui_strings` translations. Every count, here and in the sidecars, is taken one way: N is the messages whose English has the term, matched by the sidecar pin's `msgidPattern` (or its `msgid` as a whole word) where a pin holds the word, and n is those whose translation matches the pin's `expect`, without its anchors and ignoring case; a term Meru alone has, such as a device label, is counted by the Chrome term its word comes from, and a pin on Meru's own phrase or label form by the word it is built on. A ban's count is the messages whose translation has its banned form; **(Apple …)**, **(Microsoft …)** or **(GNOME …)** for an operating system's word, cited as the style sheet's Platform words cite it; **(Gmail …)**; **(Google)**; **(agent-chosen: word: n of 7 (apps))**, the last step's tally; or **(agent-chosen, unresolved by the app count: …)**, with each app's word, for a tie or a concept fewer than 3 apps have. A speaker's review replaces either. Use an agent-chosen word like any other.

[counting.md](counting.md) says how to take a Chrome count or a vote again, and when.
